On 1 January 1901 six colonies became one nation. Over the next twenty-eight years its Parliament wrote laws about who could vote, who could come in, who could be paid a pension, and who could fight. Its soldiers went to Gallipoli and France. And in 1928, in the centre of the continent, police and settlers killed Aboriginal people, and an official board said it was justified.

This file asks one question, again and again. Who was in the nation, and who decided?

How this works

Map the years from 1894 to 1928. Compare what the laws and reports said with what the other records show. Decode the words the laws use. Then investigate four questions. In each one, read what is agreed, ask three witnesses the same three questions, and write your finding.

What did they say? Could they really know? Why might they say it? “We can’t know” is always an allowed finding, if you can say why.

Every quotation is a real recorded word, from the source named under it. Where we have a document only through someone else’s report, it says secondhand. Nothing in this file is imagined. The See the source buttons open the original online.

Before you start

This file includes deaths in war and the killing of Aboriginal people at Coniston. It names and counts the dead; it does not describe them or show them. Some old documents use words that are offensive today. They are quoted as they were written. If anything here is upsetting, stop and talk to your teacher.

    Section 41, 1900 to 1902

    What the paper said

    The Constitution · section 41

    “No adult person who has or acquires a right to vote at elections for the more numerous House of the Parliament of a State shall, while the right continues, be prevented by any law of the Commonwealth from voting at elections for either House of the Parliament of the Commonwealth.”

    Source. Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act 1900 (UK), s. 41.

    See the source (Documenting a Democracy) ↗

    What the records say happened

    Parliament of South Australia · Voting History in SA · today

    “This should have protected the voting rights of Aboriginal people in South Australia, as they could already vote. But over the years, debate on the meaning of these laws, and decisions of electoral officials, resulted in many Aboriginal people losing their right to vote in Federal elections.”

    Source. Parliament of South Australia, “Voting History in SA”. A modern summary, not a document of the time.

    See the source (Parliament of SA) ↗

    Section 41 protected people who could already vote in a State. Did it protect Aboriginal voters in South Australia?

    Does the record match the paper?

    The dictation test, 1901

    What the paper said

    Immigration Restriction Act 1901 · section 3

    “(a) Any person who when asked to do so by an officer fails to write out at dictation and sign in the presence of the officer a passage of fifty words in length in an European language directed by the officer …”

    Source. Immigration Restriction Act 1901 (Cth), No. 17 of 1901, s. 3(a).

    See the source (Documenting a Democracy) ↗

    What the records say happened

    The figures · 1902 to 1909

    In 1902 and 1903 the test was given 805 times. Forty-six people passed. After 1909, nobody passed it.

    Source. Museum of Australian Democracy, Documenting a Democracy, history page for the Act. Secondhand: the departmental returns were not seen.

    The Act called it a test of writing. Do the figures look like a test of writing?

    Who could enlist, 1916

    What the paper said

    Instructions to enlisting officers · 1916

    “Aboriginals, half-casts, or men with Asiatic blood are not to be enlisted – This applies to all coloured men.”

    Source. Australian War Memorial, “Indigenous service”. Secondhand: quoted by the Australian War Memorial; the original was not seen.

    See the source (AWM) ↗

    What the records say happened

    What the records show

    Some Aboriginal men enlisted anyway. Richard Martin, born on Stradbroke Island, gave his birthplace as Dunedin, New Zealand, and joined the 15th Battalion at Gallipoli in May 1915. The Australian War Memorial believes 70 Aboriginal men served at Gallipoli, and about 1,000 Indigenous Australians served in the war.

    Source. Australian War Memorial, “Indigenous service” and “Aboriginal presence on Gallipoli grows” (secondhand summaries).

    The instructions said “not to be enlisted”. What do the records show?

    Words on the record

    The people who wrote the laws and the reports chose their words. Some words hide more than they show. Tap each card to decode it.

    • The phrase the Constitution and many Commonwealth laws used for Aboriginal people. The same words, “aboriginal native of”, were also used for people of Asia, Africa and the Pacific Islands.

      Source. Constitution s. 127; Commonwealth Franchise Act 1902, s. 4; Maternity Allowance Act 1912, s. 6.

    • Counting. Section 127 said that “in reckoning the numbers of the people”, “aboriginal natives shall not be counted”.

      Source. Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act 1900 (UK), s. 127.

    • A person not allowed to enter Australia. The Act listed classes of prohibited immigrant. The first was anyone who failed the dictation test.

      Source. Immigration Restriction Act 1901, s. 3.

    • The test passage had to be in a European language. The officer chose which one.

      Source. Immigration Restriction Act 1901, s. 3(a).

    • A word the laws used for people from Asia. The pension law excepted Asiatics born in Australia. The maternity law did not.

      Source. Invalid and Old-age Pensions Act 1908, s. 16; Maternity Allowance Act 1912, s. 6.

    • The test for who had to do compulsory training in peacetime. Medical officers were to “be the judges” of it.

      Source. Defence Act 1909, s. 138.

    • An offensive term of the time for a person with one Aboriginal and one European parent. The army’s rules used it to decide who could enlist. It is quoted here as written.

      Source. Military Order 200 of 1917; AWM, “Indigenous service” (secondhand).

    • The paper first said the two gunmen “could be plainly seen to be either Turks or Afghans”. Because of the flag, it was “assumed that they were Turks”. The same day, under the headline “Turks Identified”, the paper called both men Turks.

      Source. Barrier Miner, 1 January 1915, pp. 2 and 4.

    • People living in Australia who were citizens of countries at war with Britain, such as Germany and Turkey. The paper reported that more arrests were “expected”.

      Source. “At the Sultan’s Command”, The Argus, 6 January 1915.

    • Attacks on property, such as spearing cattle. The Board said there was “no provocation by settlers to account for depredations of aborigines”.

      Source. Northern Territory Times, 5 February 1929, p. 3.

    • One of the Board’s listed causes of the trouble was “unattached missionaries … preaching a doctrine of equality”.

      Source. Northern Territory Times, 5 February 1929, p. 3.

    • Cleared of blame. The paper’s headline was “Police Exonerated”.

      Source. Northern Territory Times, 5 February 1929, p. 3.

    Who Counts, 1902

    Who did the new nation count as its people?

    Draft, pending consultation

    What is agreed

    • On 1 January 1901 the six colonies became the Commonwealth of Australia, under a Constitution approved by the voters of the colonies and passed by the British Parliament.
    • South Australia had given women the vote in 1894, with no words about race. Western Australia gave women the vote in 1899, but barred “aboriginal native” people from the roll unless they owned land.
    • On 12 June 1902 the Commonwealth Franchise Act set out who could vote for the new Parliament.
    • Between 1903 and 1912 the same groups of words appeared in laws about naturalization, pensions, defence and the maternity allowance.

    Ask every witness the same three questions: What did they say? Could they really know? Why might they say it? Some witnesses here are documents, not people. Tap a picture to enlarge it.

    The Constitution

    Section 127 · 1900

    Source. Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act 1900 (UK), s. 127.

    See the source (Documenting a Democracy) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “In reckoning the numbers of the people of the Commonwealth, or of a State or other part of the Commonwealth, aboriginal natives shall not be counted.”

    Could they really have seen it? It is the founding law itself. It does not describe the people; it sets a rule for counting them.

    Why might they say it? It was written by delegates from the colonies in the 1890s. No Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander person was among them. The count decided each State’s share of seats.

    The Commonwealth Franchise Act

    Sections 3 and 4 · 12 June 1902

    Source. Commonwealth Franchise Act 1902 (Cth), No. 8 of 1902, ss. 3–4.

    See the source (Documenting a Democracy) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “all persons not under twenty-one years of age whether male or female married or unmarried— (a) Who have lived in Australia for six months continuously, and (b) Who are natural born or naturalized subjects of the King, and (c) Whose names are on the Electoral Roll for any Electoral Division, shall be entitled to vote …”

    “No aboriginal native of Australia Asia Africa or the Islands of the Pacific except New Zealand shall be entitled to have his name placed on an Electoral Roll unless so entitled under section forty-one of the Constitution.”

    Could they really have seen it? It is the law. It sets out exactly who may be enrolled, and who may not, unless section 41 protects them.

    Why might they say it? It gave the vote to women across the country in the same Act that took it from most Aboriginal people. Why might one law do both?

    The Parliament of South Australia

    “Voting History in SA” · a modern summary

    Source. Parliament of South Australia, education page, “Voting History in SA”.

    See the source (Parliament of SA) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “This should have protected the voting rights of Aboriginal people in South Australia, as they could already vote. But over the years, debate on the meaning of these laws, and decisions of electoral officials, resulted in many Aboriginal people losing their right to vote in Federal elections.”

    Could they really have seen it? It is a parliament describing its own history, many years later. It summarises; it does not quote the officials’ decisions.

    Why might they say it? A parliament has reasons to tell its history carefully, and to be proud of 1894. Here it records a failure.

    The Dictation Test, 1901

    Was the test about language?

    What is agreed

    • The Immigration Restriction Act was signed on 23 December 1901. It did not name any race.
    • It listed classes of “prohibited immigrant”. The first was anyone who failed a dictation test of fifty words in a European language.
    • The officer chose the passage and the language.

    Try the rule

    Read section 3(a) below. You are the officer. A traveller arrives who speaks and writes three languages well. Which language would you choose for the test, if you wanted them to pass? If you wanted them to fail?

    Ask three witnesses

    Ask every witness the same three questions: What did they say? Could they really know? Why might they say it? Some witnesses here are documents, not people. Tap a picture to enlarge it.

    The Immigration Restriction Act

    Section 3 · 23 December 1901

    Source. Immigration Restriction Act 1901 (Cth), No. 17 of 1901, s. 3(a).

    See the source (Documenting a Democracy) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “(a) Any person who when asked to do so by an officer fails to write out at dictation and sign in the presence of the officer a passage of fifty words in length in an European language directed by the officer …”

    Could they really have seen it? It is the law itself. It says what the test was, but not who it was meant for.

    Why might they say it? The Act does not say why it named no one. Compare it with the third witness.

    The figures

    Tests given, 1902 to 1909

    Source. Museum of Australian Democracy, Documenting a Democracy, history page for the Act. Secondhand: the departmental returns were not seen.

    What did they say they saw? In 1902 and 1903 the test was given 805 times. Forty-six people passed. After 1909, nobody passed it.

    Could they really have seen it? They are a later summary of official returns. They count tests, not the people who never tried to come.

    Why might they say it? Figures do not have motives, but the people who chose when to give the test did.

    The same Parliament, a year later

    Commonwealth Franchise Act · section 4 · 1902

    Source. Commonwealth Franchise Act 1902 (Cth), s. 4.

    See the source (Documenting a Democracy) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “No aboriginal native of Australia Asia Africa or the Islands of the Pacific except New Zealand shall be entitled to have his name placed on an Electoral Roll unless so entitled under section forty-one of the Constitution.”

    Could they really have seen it? It is a law passed by the same Parliament, six months after the Immigration Restriction Act.

    Why might they say it? This law names peoples openly. The Immigration Restriction Act did not. Why might one law name them and the other not?

    Gallipoli and Fromelles, 1915 to 1918

    Who was allowed to serve, and what did they come home to?

    Draft, pending consultation

    What is agreed

    • On 25 April 1915 Australian and New Zealand troops landed at Gallipoli. They were taken off on 19 and 20 December. The campaign cost 26,111 Australian casualties, including 8,141 deaths.
    • On 19 July 1916 the 5th Australian Division attacked at Fromelles, in France. Over 5,500 Australians became casualties in a night and a morning.
    • Overseas service was by volunteering. Voters said no to conscription twice, in 1916 and 1917.
    • The rules for the AIF kept out most men who were not “of European origin”. Some Aboriginal men enlisted anyway. The Australian War Memorial estimates about 1,000 Indigenous Australians served.

    Ellis Ashmead-Bartlett

    British war correspondent · report of the landing · Sydney Morning Herald, 8 May 1915

    Source. Sydney Morning Herald, 8 May 1915, p. 13.

    See the source (Trove) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “The Australians rose to the occasion. They did not wait for orders or for the boats to reach the beach, but sprang into the sea, formed a sort of rough line, and rushed the enemy’s trenches.”

    “There has been no finer feat in this war than this sudden landing in the dark and the storming of the heights, and above all, the holding on whilst reinforcements were landing.”

    Could they really have seen it? His report was printed in Sydney on 8 May, thirteen days after the landing. Read it closely: does he say where he was when he saw it?

    Why might they say it? He was writing for a public at war, and his report helped to shape how the landing was remembered. He calls the Australians a “race of athletes”. Who was counted in that “race”?

    Private Richard Martin

    Enlisted December 1914 · 15th Battalion

    Source. Australian War Memorial, “Indigenous service”. Secondhand: the AWM’s summary of his service record.

    What did they say they saw? His attestation papers said he was born in Dunedin, New Zealand. He was born on Stradbroke Island, Queensland.     Mitcham camp, South Australia · June 1917

    Source. Australian War Memorial, “Indigenous service”. Secondhand: the medical board’s words as quoted by the AWM.

    Harry Thorpe was born at the Lake Tyers mission station in Victoria. He enlisted at Sale in February 1916, was wounded at Pozières and Bullecourt, and won the Military Medal at Broodseinde in October 1917. He was shot on 9 August 1918 and died shortly afterwards. He is buried in France beside his friend William Rawlings, another Aboriginal winner of the Military Medal, killed the same day.

    Source. Australian War Memorial, “Indigenous service” (secondhand summary).

    When Thorpe enlisted in 1916, what did the Defence Act and the 1916 instructions say? What did the Franchise Act say about his vote?

    Investigation

    Coniston, 1928

    What did the Board of Enquiry find, and what did it leave out?

    Draft, pending consultation

    Before you start

    This investigation is about the killing of Aboriginal people in 1928. It names and counts the dead; it does not describe them. Warlpiri, Anmatyerr and Kaytetye families still remember Coniston. If anything here is upsetting, stop and talk to your teacher.

    What is agreed

    • Coniston was a cattle station north-west of Alice Springs, on Warlpiri, Anmatyerr and Kaytetye Country. In 1928 the region had been in drought for five years.
    • On 7 August 1928 a dingo trapper, Fred Brooks, was killed at Yurrkuru, near the Coniston homestead.
    • Mounted Constable George Murray, a veteran of the Boer War and the First World War, led armed parties through the region in the weeks that followed.
    • Two Aboriginal men were tried in Darwin for Brooks’s murder, and acquitted.
    • A Board of Enquiry of three officials reported in January 1929.

    Ask three witnesses

    Ask every witness the same three questions: What did they say? Could they really know? Why might they say it? Some witnesses here are documents, not people. Tap a picture to enlarge it.

    Constable Murray, as reported by the Rev. Stanley Jarvis

    A Methodist minister, Darwin · sermon reported in the Daily News (Perth), 13 November 1928

    Source. “Native Slaughter”, Daily News (Perth), 13 November 1928, p. 6.

    See the source (Trove) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “Mounted-constable Murray admitted that he and his party shot down 17 aborigines at odd times during the 20 days’ hunt for the alleged murderers.”

    “The constable said he shot to kill.”

    “Who will take them back to their own country? Will they ever get back? Judge Mallam remarked during the recital of the tragic events that the police mowed them down wholesale.”

    Could they really have seen it? Jarvis was reporting what Murray and the judge had said in court. We have Murray’s evidence only as Jarvis retold it in a sermon, and the paper reported it from Darwin.

    Why might they say it? Jarvis said it “would not be right for him as a missionary … to keep silent”. He also asked: “It looks as if they were shot down at different places”

    The Board of Enquiry

    Report released January 1929 · as summarised in the Northern Territory Times, 5 February 1929

    Source. “Tragedy of the Centre. Police Exonerated”, Northern Territory Times, 5 February 1929, p. 3. The full report was not seen.

    See the source (Trove) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “It says that in each case the shooting was justified and that there was no provocation by settlers to account for depredations of aborigines.”

    “The Board was prepared to believe the evidence of all witnesses.”

    “(B) Unattached missionaries wandering from place to place, having no previous knowledge of the blacks, and preaching a doctrine of equality. (C) Inexperienced white settlers making free with natives and treating them as equals.”

    Could they really have seen it? Its members were a magistrate, the Government Resident and a police inspector. The summary says Murray was “corroborated by three reputable settlers and a tracker”.

    Why might they say it? It was judging the actions of a police party, and one of its three members was a police inspector.

    Broken Hill, in brief

    Draft, pending consultation

    On New Year’s Day 1915 two men fired on the Broken Hill picnic train. The Barrier Miner described it.

    Barrier Miner · 1 January 1915

    “The train consisted of two break vans and 40 ore trucks of the usual sort used for Barrier picnics, with a freight of some 1200 picnickers on board.”
    “The flag was red about 18in. square, with a white cresent and white star—the flag of Turkey.”

    Source. “War in Broken Hill”, Barrier Miner, 1 January 1915, p. 2. Spelling as printed.

    See the source (Trove) ↗

    Four people were killed and seven wounded. Both gunmen, Gool Mahomed and Mulla Abdulla, were killed. During the fight, a camel owner from Peshawar helped a wounded police officer.

    Barrier Miner · 1 January 1915

    “Assou is a British subject and has a brother in the British army, whilst he himself is a camel owner. His house is near to the scene of the fight and when one of the police officers was shot, he carried water to him.”

    Source. “Turks Identified”, Barrier Miner, 1 January 1915, p. 4.

    See the source (Trove) ↗

    Notes from the two men were found among the rocks, and roughly translated.

    The Argus · 6 January 1915 · a rough translation

    Gool Mahomed: “I am a poor man, and belong to the Sultan … Fight and tell your people, because your people are fighting my country … I have no enemies among you and nobody (else) has told me to do this …”
    Mulla Abdulla: “I have been worried because I have been fined, and I have brooded over it … I have never worn a turban since the day some larrikins threw stones at me, and I did not like it I wear the turban to-day …”

    Source. “At the Sultan’s Command”, The Argus (Melbourne), 6 January 1915. Punctuation as printed.
